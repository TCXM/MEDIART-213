# Common Ground — Design Reflection

Common Ground imagines a city in 2040 where people and robots share everyday life. Instead of presenting the future as a list of inventions, this prototype gives visitors a place to explore. Six locations connect food, learning, movement, ecology, culture, and care. The central question is not how capable a machine can become, but what people could do together with its support.

The kitchen provides a concrete example. A robot helps lift a pot while a resident directs the meal. Someone who finds heavy cookware difficult can still contribute knowledge and taste. The care courtyard follows the same idea: carrying a bag is useful, but listening remains human work. These scenes imagine support for older residents, caregivers, students, and people with different physical abilities and resources.

The map changes how the story is encountered. Visitors can choose a destination rather than follow a fixed reading order. A dark teal palette, surveyed grid, isometric buildings, and moving transit suggest a technological city. Warm accents distinguish its shared places. Each landmark opens a miniature scene with three moments: arriving, joining an activity, and considering its wider implications. Short captions connect the visual experience to a question rather than declaring that the future is solved.

p5.js draws the buildings, people, and robots from simple shapes. This keeps the visual language consistent and makes the artwork part of the code I can study and modify. Twelve smaller street stories reward zooming in: drying laundry, repairing a radio, sharing books, or playing chess. HTML buttons provide access beyond canvas interaction. Keyboard navigation, touch gestures, and optional animation make exploration more flexible. The scenes remain illustrations, not demonstrations of working robotic systems.

The imagined city also depends on shared equipment, paid maintenance, accessible homes, and places where people can gather without buying anything. These conditions matter as much as automation. My responsibility as a designer is to make technology feel approachable while leaving its social choices visible. The prototype was created with coding assistance and represents a possible direction, not a prediction.
